# Intrinsic one-hole divisors and exact factorization of covering sets

Codex (OpenAI), at Jordan Pollard's request, November 28, 2026. AI-authored research for public review on Erdős #1189.

Given one covering assignment for an irreducible modulus set, a directed graph identifies **every** divisor of a reserved modulus on which the other rows can be reassigned to leave a single residue. When the reserved modulus is the full period, the partially ordered set of strongly connected components and the component owning each row are independent of the assignment. In particular the component carrying a hypothetical fixed logarithmic gain can be chosen once for the modulus set. The second theorem gives an exact factorization for arbitrary reserved moduli and overlapping periods. We allow repeated moduli as separately indexed rows in factors. Original modulus sets have distinct moduli greater than one.

## 1. Recovering all one-hole divisors from one assignment

Represent residues modulo the period L by all their CRT prime digits. A row tests a set of digits, fixing exactly one tuple on this support. Choose any reserved row with support R and any successful assignment. Choose any point covered only by this row and call its digit labels zero. Such a point exists by irreducibility. In each ordinary row draw arrows from every tested nonzero digit to every tested digit of the row. Here and below, a nonzero digit means a position at which this row requires a label different from zero.

For a set of positions U inside R, use all ordinary rows with support contained in U and let h(U) be the minimum number of uncovered tuples on U over every reassignment. For the empty set the one empty tuple is uncovered. Then
$$
  h(U)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \text{no arrow leaves }U.
$$
In the affirmative case the original internal rows already miss just zero on U. The quantity h(U) is always positive because a full cover of U by its internal rows would be a proper subcover.

To prove the forward implication, place the internal rows to leave exactly zero on U, using independent label permutations to normalize their hole. Keep all other original rows and the reserved row unchanged. On the zero fibre of U, the original internal rows were inactive because they missed the private zero point. Thus the unchanged rows suffice there. Off this fibre the replaced internal rows suffice. If a crossing row required a nonzero label inside U, it would be unnecessary throughout this new cover, in contradiction to irreducibility under reassignment. Conversely, if no arrow leaves U, extend any nonzero tuple on U by zeros outside it. It misses the reserved row. An ordinary row hitting it has some nonzero requirement, necessarily within U, so its entire support is inside U. This proves the equivalence.

Closed subsets are precisely the ideals in the reachability preorder, with arrow targets treated as prerequisites. For a reserved row smaller than the period, discard vertices from which a vertex outside R is reachable. The remaining vertices form the largest eligible subset, and its ideals give all the answers. Every such ideal really is a divisor: a digit in R has an ordinary row hitting the point that changes just that digit, so all lower positions at the same prime are arrow targets. Consequently the family of divisors is closed under gcd and lcm; its largest element and the prerequisite relation on it are intrinsic. Given the digits, one strongly connected component computation and a search backwards from the outside of R construct this representation. The number of ideals can itself be exponential.

If R is the full period, the graph's *entire transitive closure* is intrinsic: j is reachable from i exactly when every feasible set containing i contains j. In a row all nonzero requirements lie in one component C, and all its other positions lie in C or below C. Thus C is the unique greatest component tested by its support and is intrinsic as well. The actual arrows of a witness need not be recovered by this test. This strengthens the [earlier fixed-witness component decomposition](component-surplus.md). The argument also applies directly to products of arbitrary finite alphabets, with no prime-prefix requirement.

## 2. A cut into two ordinary integer problems, without a full-period assumption

Partition an indexed modulus list D into nonempty lists I and O. Put
$$
 P=\mathop{\rm lcm}(I),\qquad Q=\mathop{\rm lcm}(O),\qquad g=\gcd(P,Q).
$$
The inside factor J is I augmented by one moveable row of modulus g. The outside factor K consists of the indexed moduli n/gcd(n,P) for n in O. A modulus 1 is an allowed universal row in these tests. The natural interface between the two periods is a residue modulo g.

**Exact split.** If J is coverable, D is irreducible if and only if both J and K are irreducible. More generally, for any *given irreducible D*, J is coverable if and only if K is irreducible. Therefore exactly one of the following holds for each cut of an irreducible cover. Either it factors into the two irreducible lists, or K is coverable with a proper subfamily and every placement of I misses at least two different boundary residues modulo g. In an exact split the two defects add, with defect defined as size minus one minus the sum of p−1 over all prime positions in the list's period.

Indeed I cannot cover in any irreducible D. Restrict a successful placement to a P-residue h missed by I. The active O rows induce a cover by a sublist of K, using the substitution x=h+Pm. If K is irreducible, **all** O rows are active on **every** such h. Their compatibility conditions on h have moduli gcd(n,P) with lcm g. Hence all I holes have one common g-residue. This proves that J covers. Conversely, whenever I can confine its holes to one g-residue, any cover by K lifts to a cover of those holes. Choose a representative h and lift the rows independently on h modulo P. Every other required P-fibre is a translate of this one by a multiple of Q, by the CRT. Thus all lifted rows still cover there. In particular reducibility of K gives a proper subcover of D. If I could cover outright, D and J would both be reducible whenever D is under consideration as an irreducible candidate.

For the full equivalence when J covers, if D is irreducible the preceding argument makes K irreducible. J cannot lose its virtual row, since I cannot cover. Nor can it lose an inside row: the diminished confinement and the intact quotient would cover D. Conversely rigid J and K supply a cover by gluing. In any proposed proper subcover of D, the remaining I rows alone cannot cover. Restriction to an old hole forces all O rows by rigidity of K. All inside holes must then share a boundary residue as above, forcing all I rows by rigidity of J. The period of K is lcm(P,Q)/P, which gives defect additivity by summing exponents. These implications hold for freely changed residues on either side. Confined holes may be numerous modulo P.

## 3. A surplus on one intrinsically indecomposable integer quotient

Consider a full-period irreducible set D of k distinct moduli. Write S for its reciprocal sum and $\Phi(L)=\sum_{p^j\mid L,\ j\ge1}H_{p-1}/p^j$. Fix $\epsilon>0$ and suppose $S-\Phi(L)\ge\epsilon\log k$ for arbitrarily large k. By [component concentration](component-concentration.md) there is a component C whose own reciprocal mass minus its ideal mass is at least $\nu\log k$, for a constant $\nu(\epsilon)>0$. Section 1 now makes this same choice work in every successful assignment.

Let P consist of the digits in strict predecessors of C, let PQ also include C, and assign rows to their greatest component. Both sets of digits are ideals. On the P-fibre every C-row with original modulus n has integer modulus $m_n=n/\gcd(n,P)$ after alignment. These local rows, with indices kept, can leave one hole modulo Q; they can never cover Q, and after any row deletion they can never leave fewer than two holes. This is the component factorization, or follows by plugging a better local placement into a topological ordering with all earlier guards aligned. For every proper divisor $1<T<Q$ the local rows dividing T always miss **at least two** T-residues. Otherwise one could first align the predecessor subsystem at a single P-residue, then align the T-subsystem above it. This would give a one-hole period PT strictly between P and PQ containing only part of C, contrary to the ideal characterization. Unused divisor-rows cannot fill this hole since the ordinary full-period list is noncoverable. C occupies a consecutive interval of positions at each of its primes, by the prime-prefix arrows.

For clarity the quantitative conclusion keeps the *original* reciprocal weights and cutoffs. Fix $\sigma>0$, allow erasure of rows of mass at most $\lambda\log k$, and put $\mu=\nu-\sigma/2-\lambda>0$. Set $b_p=\lfloor(p-1)p^\sigma\rfloor$, $B(T)=\sum_{p^j\mid T,\ j\ge1} b_p$. At a set of $x\in[k^{\mu/8},k]$ of logarithmic measure at least $(\mu/3)\log k$, a nested choice $T_x\mid Q$ satisfies
$$
 \#\{n\text{ surviving at C}: n\le x,\ m_n\mid T_x\}
             \ \ge\ B(T_x)+(\mu/8)x.
$$
The counted rows can fill all the indicated digit slots with distinct original moduli and leave the displayed linear surplus. This is inside one primitive factor; no further proper nontrivial one-hole divisor contraction exists even if every row is restored. The possibility $T_x=Q$ is included.

Indeed scan the surviving rows of C by *original n*, matching each to slots at digits of C it tests. Distinct original n assigned to a digit whose original position is $(p,j)$ are different multiples of $p^j$, with mass at most $H_{b_p}/p^j$. Thus the total matched mass is at most $(1+\sigma)\phi_C$, where $\phi_C$ is the ideal mass of C and $\phi_C\le(1/2+o(1))\log k$ by the classical period budget. The rejected mass is at least $(\mu-o(1))\log k$. The prefix deficiency is the maximum of the left count minus $B(T)$ over $T\mid Q$, using the initial segments of *local* digits. The largest maximizers are nested by supermodularity. Integration of this deficiency against $dx/x^2$, exactly as in [the Hall-period proof](nested-hall-periods.md), gives the asserted scale and matching bounds. Using $1/m_n$ in this calculation would lose original distinctness. The contractions above give indexed systems. Bounding their possible mass with their actual distinct original dressings is the surviving obstacle.
